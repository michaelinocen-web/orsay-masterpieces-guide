Musee d'Orsay

Must-see masterpieces for a 1.5-2 hour visit

Suggested route: Head straight to the top floor first (escalators near the entrance) - that's where most of the essential Impressionist and Post-Impressionist works live. Come back down to the ground floor for the Manets if time allows. Skip the middle level and decorative arts sections given the time window.
Ground Floor

Olympia - Edouard Manet (1863)

Ground floor

A nude woman stares directly at the viewer, unashamed, while a maid presents her flowers from a client - clearly a courtesan, not a goddess. Manet based the pose on Titian's Venus of Urbino, but stripped away the mythological alibi. Shown at the 1865 Salon, it scandalized Paris - not for nudity itself, but for her confrontational gaze and the frank suggestion of paid sex. It needed guards to protect it from angry viewers.

Le Dejeuner sur l'herbe - Edouard Manet (1863)

Ground floor

A nude woman picnics with two fully dressed men in a park while another bathes in the background - with no mythological or historical excuse for the nudity. Rejected by the official Salon, it was shown at the 1863 "Salon des Refuses," a separate exhibition Napoleon III authorized after so many artists were rejected that year. It became a founding gesture of modern painting: ordinary life, painted flatly and boldly, refusing academic convention.

The Gleaners - Jean-Francois Millet (1857)

Ground floor

Three peasant women bend to collect leftover grain after harvest - the lowest, most menial rural labor. Millet monumentalized them like classical statues, which unsettled critics used to seeing peasants either idealized or ignored; some saw a political statement about class and rural poverty in post-1848 France. A cornerstone of Realism, treating ordinary working people with the seriousness usually reserved for saints and heroes.

Top Floor - Impressionist & Post-Impressionist Galleries

Starry Night Over the Rhone - Vincent van Gogh (1888)

Top floor

Painted in Arles, looking out over the river at night, gas lamps reflecting on the water beneath a swirling starlit sky. Van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo about his fascination with painting at night, straight from life under the stars - this predates his more famous The Starry Night (MoMA) by about a year and is generally considered calmer and more observational.

Self-Portraits & Bedroom in Arles - Vincent van Gogh (1888)

Top floor

The self-portraits span his years in Paris and Arles, tracking his shifting palette and mental state. Bedroom in Arles depicts his room in the "Yellow House," painted with deliberately flattened perspective and bold color - he wanted the simplicity to convey rest, though the tilted walls also hint at his instability. He made three versions; check the wall label for which one is on display.

Rouen Cathedral series - Claude Monet

Top floor

Monet painted the cathedral's facade over 30 times from a rented room across the square, at different hours and light conditions, to capture how light itself - not the object - creates what we see. One of the purest statements of Impressionist theory: the subject is almost incidental to the study of atmosphere and time of day.

Bal du moulin de la Galette - Pierre-Auguste Renoir (1876)

Top floor

An outdoor Sunday-afternoon dance in Montmartre, packed with Parisians in dappled sunlight. Renoir hauled a large canvas to the actual site and painted it largely on location with friends as models - remarkable given its size. It captures Impressionism's ideal: modern leisure, ordinary people, fleeting light, painted with visible, energetic brushwork.

Ballet Dancers & The Absinthe Drinker - Edgar Degas (1876)

Top floor

Degas obsessively studied dancers backstage and mid-rehearsal - sweat and awkward angles, not idealized grace. The Absinthe Drinker shows a man and woman slumped at a cafe table, isolated even next to each other, absinthe glass in front of her - a bleak, modern image of urban alienation and addiction that shocked audiences with its unglamorous honesty.

Card Players & Still Lifes - Paul Cezanne

Top floor

The Card Players series (Orsay has one version; others are in different museums) depicts peasant men absorbed in a quiet game - stripped of narrative drama, focused on solid geometric form. His still lifes similarly treat apples and drapery as studies in structure, famously influencing the shift toward Cubism decades later.

Pointillist works - Georges Seurat

Top floor, if on display

Seurat developed "pointillism" - building images from tiny dots of pure color that blend optically in the viewer's eye rather than on the canvas, based on contemporary color theory.

If You Have Extra Minutes

Whistler's Mother - James McNeill Whistler (1871)

Formally: Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1

Whistler insisted this was primarily a study in composition and tone, not a sentimental portrait of his mother - the title tells you how he wanted it read. It became one of the most reproduced and parodied images in Western art, ironically for exactly the sentimental reasons he downplayed.

The Clock Window

Top floor

The museum was once the Gare d'Orsay, a Beaux-Arts railway station built for the 1900 World's Fair. The giant iron-and-glass clock is a remnant of that original structure - now framing a view of Paris and a great photo spot, sitting right in the Impressionist galleries.

A quick-reference guide for a short visit to the Musee d'Orsay, Paris.
