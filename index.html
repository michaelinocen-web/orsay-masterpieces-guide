Musée d'Orsay A Complete Masterpieces Guide Prepared for Michael · Visit length: 1.5–2 hours · Entry: Seine-side entrance Visited 0 / 15 How This Guide Works This route is built for minimum backtracking: it starts in the ground-floor Millet and Manet rooms nearest the entrance, then rides the escalators up to the top floor for a single clockwise loop through the Impressionist and Post-Impressionist galleries, ending at the Clock Window. The full route covers 15 works. If you only have 90 minutes, do stops 1, 3, 6–9, and 15 — that's the tightest "don't miss" core along the same path, no extra doubling back. Getting to the Museum Address: 1 Rue de la Légion d'Honneur, 75007 Paris (Esplanade Valéry Giscard d'Estaing entrance) Google Maps: Musée d'Orsay — open in Google Maps RER: Musée d'Orsay (RER C), steps from the entrance Metro: Solférino (Line 12), 5-minute walk Bus: Lines 63, 68, 69, 73, 83, 84, 94 The museum occupies the former Gare d'Orsay, a Beaux-Arts railway station built for the 1900 World's Fair. Escalators at the far end of the ground floor run straight up to the top-floor Impressionist galleries. The Full Route 01 The Gleaners Jean-François Millet · 1857 · Ground floor The Gleaners Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Start in the ground-floor Realism and Barbizon-school rooms, close to the entrance. Three peasant women bend to collect leftover grain after harvest — the lowest, most menial rural labor. Millet monumentalized them like classical statues, which unsettled critics used to seeing peasants either idealized or ignored. Why it matters: A cornerstone of Realism, treating ordinary working people with the seriousness usually reserved for saints and heroes. 02 The Angelus Jean-François Millet · 1857–1859 · Ground floor The Angelus Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Same rooms as The Gleaners — it hangs nearby among Millet's other Barbizon-school work. Two peasants pause at dusk over a basket of potatoes to say the Angelus prayer as a distant church bell rings. Millet originally called it "Prayer for the Potato Harvest" and added the steeple on the horizon later, renaming it after the prayer itself. Why it matters: It became one of the most reproduced religious images of the 19th century, and later obsessed Salvador Dalí, who was convinced (without real evidence) that an X-ray showed a buried coffin hidden beneath the figures' feet. 03 Olympia Édouard Manet · 1863 · Ground floor Olympia Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Continue further along the ground floor toward the Manet rooms, which lead on to the escalators up to the Impressionist galleries. A nude woman stares directly at the viewer, unashamed, while a maid presents her flowers from a client — clearly a courtesan, not a goddess. Manet based the pose on Titian's Venus of Urbino, but stripped away the mythological alibi. Shown at the 1865 Salon, it scandalized Paris — not for nudity itself, but for her confrontational gaze and the frank suggestion of paid sex. Why it matters: It's often cited as the painting that launched modern art by refusing to dress up its subject in myth. 04 Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe Édouard Manet · 1863 · Ground floor* Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Just beside Olympia, in the same room. Note: sources disagree on this — some visitor guides place it on the top floor with the other Manets instead, so check the wall label on the day. A nude woman picnics with two fully dressed men in a park while another bathes in the background — with no mythological or historical excuse for the nudity. Rejected by the official Salon, it was shown at the 1863 "Salon des Refusés," a separate exhibition Napoleon III authorized after so many artists were rejected that year. Why it matters: A founding gesture of modern painting: ordinary life, painted flatly and boldly, refusing academic convention. 05 The Fifer Édouard Manet · 1866 · Ground floor The Fifer Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Same Manet rooms — this is the last ground-floor stop before heading up. A young military musician stands isolated against a flat, undefined grey background, rendered with almost no shadow or spatial depth. Rejected by the 1866 Salon jury, critics mocked it for looking as flat as a playing card. Why it matters: That flatness — influenced by Japanese woodblock prints Manet was collecting — became one of the key steps toward abandoning traditional illusionistic depth. 06 The Floor Scrapers Gustave Caillebotte · 1875 · Top floor The Floor Scrapers Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Take the escalators at the far end of the ground floor up to the top-floor Impressionist galleries. This hangs near the entrance to those rooms, bridging Realism and Impressionism. Three shirtless laborers scrape a Parisian apartment floor bare, their bodies caught in raking afternoon light. Rejected by the 1875 Salon jury as too coarse a subject for serious painting, Caillebotte showed it instead at the second Impressionist exhibition in 1876. Why it matters: One of the first major paintings to treat urban manual labor with the formal seriousness usually reserved for history painting, built on precise, almost photographic perspective rather than loose brushwork. 07 Rouen Cathedral series Claude Monet · 1892–1894 · Top floor Rouen Cathedral series Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Moving clockwise through the top floor, into the Monet rooms. Monet painted the cathedral's façade over 30 times from a rented room across the square, at different hours and light conditions, to capture how light itself — not the object — creates what we see. The Orsay's version is titled plein soleil, harmonie bleue et or (full sun, blue and gold harmony); other versions from the same series hang in Washington and Boston. Why it matters: One of the purest statements of Impressionist theory: the subject is almost incidental to the study of atmosphere and time of day. 08 Bal du moulin de la Galette Pierre-Auguste Renoir · 1876 · Top floor Bal du moulin de la Galette Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Continue clockwise into the Renoir room. An outdoor Sunday-afternoon dance in Montmartre, packed with Parisians in dappled sunlight. Renoir hauled a large canvas to the actual site and painted much of it on location with friends as models. Why it matters: Captures Impressionism's ideal: modern leisure, ordinary people, fleeting light, painted with visible, energetic brushwork. 09 Starry Night Over the Rhône Vincent van Gogh · 1888 · Top floor Starry Night Over the Rhône Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Continue clockwise into the dedicated Van Gogh room. Painted in Arles, looking out over the river at night, gas lamps reflecting on the water beneath a swirling starlit sky. Van Gogh wrote to his brother Theo about painting straight from life under the stars — this predates his more famous Starry Night (MoMA) by about a year. Why it matters: Generally considered calmer and more observational than the MoMA painting, and a rare nighttime plein-air work. 10 Self-Portraits & Bedroom in Arles Vincent van Gogh · 1888–1889 · Top floor Self-Portraits & Bedroom in Arles Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Same room as Starry Night. The self-portraits span his years in Paris and Arles, tracking his shifting palette and mental state. Bedroom in Arles depicts his room in the "Yellow House," painted with deliberately flattened perspective and bold color. Why it matters: He wanted the simplicity to convey rest, though the tilted walls also hint at his instability. He made three versions of the bedroom; check the wall label for which one is on display. 11 Card Players & Still Lifes Paul Cézanne · 1892–1896 · Top floor Card Players & Still Lifes Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Continuing clockwise, into the Cézanne room. The Card Players series (Orsay has one version; others are in different museums) depicts peasant men absorbed in a quiet game — stripped of narrative drama, focused on solid geometric form. Why it matters: His still lifes similarly treat apples and drapery as studies in structure, famously influencing the shift toward Cubism decades later. 12 Ballet Dancers & The Absinthe Drinker Edgar Degas · 1876 · Top floor Ballet Dancers & The Absinthe Drinker Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Continue to the Degas rooms, further along the clockwise loop. Degas obsessively studied dancers backstage and mid-rehearsal — sweat and awkward angles, not idealized grace. The Absinthe Drinker shows a man and woman slumped at a café table, isolated even next to each other. Why it matters: A bleak, modern image of urban alienation and addiction that shocked audiences with its unglamorous honesty. 13 The Circus Georges Seurat · 1891 (unfinished) · Top floor The Circus Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: Nearby, completing the clockwise loop through the Post-Impressionist rooms. Seurat's last, unfinished work, built from his pointillist technique — tiny dots of pure color that blend optically in the viewer's eye rather than on the canvas. He died suddenly at 31 while still working on it. Why it matters: A more scientific, methodical reaction against the spontaneity of Impressionism, cut short by his early death. 14 Whistler's Mother James McNeill Whistler · 1871 · Top floor Whistler's Mother Open on Google Images ↗ Directions: A short walk from Seurat, in the American and British rooms on the same floor. Formally titled Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1, Whistler insisted this was primarily a study in composition and tone, not a sentimental portrait of his mother. Why it matters: It became one of the most reproduced and parodied images in Western art, ironically for exactly the sentimental reasons he downplayed. 15 The Clock Window Gare d'Orsay, 1900 · Top floor Search on Google Images ↗ Directions: Finish here — the giant station clock overlooking the Seine is right at the end of the top-floor galleries, near the café. The museum was once the Gare d'Orsay, a Beaux-Arts railway station built for the 1900 World's Fair. The giant iron-and-glass clock is a remnant of that original structure. Why it matters: Frames a view of Paris toward the Louvre across the river, and is one of the best photo spots in the museum. Practical Notes This route is sequenced to avoid backtracking: ground floor first (Millet, then Manet, ending near the escalators), then one clockwise loop around the top floor. It covers roughly 1.5–2.5 hours depending on pace and crowds. The top floor gets busy by mid-morning, so an early entry helps. Room assignments at the Orsay shift periodically as the collection is rehung — if a painting isn't where this guide says, ask a staff member; they're used to it.
